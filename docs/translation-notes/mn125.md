## Notes

- From-scratch translation (145 segments). Structure: novice Aciravata, in a wilderness hut, tells prince Jayasena that a diligent mendicant can attain unification of mind; the prince rejects it and leaves. The Buddha: living amid sensual pleasures Jayasena cannot know what is known only by renunciation — threefold nekkhamma refutation (7.2–7.3, 8.7, 10.2), bracketed by the tamed/untamed elephant-horse-ox pair and the mountain simile (the friend at the peak sees parks, woods, meadows, lotus-ponds; ignorance is a bigger mountain). Had Aciravata had these similes, the prince would have had confidence. Then the great elephant-taming simile: an anointed king's tracker tethers a wild elephant to the royal bull elephant, brings it into the open (its clinging is the elephant wood), and the trainer — tethering it to the great post, gentle speech, food and water, pick-up/put-down, forward/back, stand/sit, then the imperturbability task with plank and lances — produces a war-ready, crookedness-and-taint-purged royal asset. Mapped onto the training: the Tathāgata arises, teaches, faith, going forth ("comes into the open" — gods and humans cling to the five sense-pleasures), then progressive guidance — virtue, sense-restraint (with the root's directive "(Cần được trình bày rộng rãi như trong Kinh Gaṇaka Moggallāna.)" kept as an instruction), abandoning the hindrances, the four establishments of mindfulness as the mind's tether-post; mindfulness without sensual thinking, the jhānas, the three knowledges, the four truths, liberation. Such a monk endures cold and heat… (mn119 formula) and is worthy of offerings (mn118 formula). Closing: an untamed elephant or monk that dies — old, middle-aged, young — is reckoned "chết khi chưa thuần hóa"; a tamed one "chết khi đã thuần hóa".
